Executive summary

The proposal replaces a legacy "Press 1" IVR for employee IT support with an LLM-powered voice concierge, migrating in phases instead of in one cutover. At a Tier-1 bank like JPMC, the path matters as much as the destination: accuracy, privacy and continuity of service cannot be traded for speed.

Three principles anchor the design:

  • Data-driven intent mapping. Historical support transcripts, not assumptions, decide which flows move first.
  • Zero-hallucination guardrails. Every answer is grounded in verified internal SOPs, and PII is masked before it reaches a model.
  • Frictionless human handoffs. When AI can't resolve an issue, a human agent receives full context, so the employee never repeats themselves.

Context and challenge

A menu-driven IVR forces employees to translate a problem into a keypad path, and it fails the moment a problem doesn't fit the menu. For internal IT support, that means frustrated callers, misrouted tickets and agents spending time on issues that should resolve in seconds, such as VPN failures and MFA resets.

Replacing it at JPMC scale is hard for reasons beyond the technology:

  • Accuracy is non-negotiable. A wrong answer about access or security is worse than no answer.
  • Sensitive data is in every call. Employee identifiers and account details can't flow freely into third-party models.
  • Support can't go dark. Agents in ServiceNow and Genesys keep working while the new front door is built beside the old one.
  • Trust has to be earned. A big-bang replacement invites a failure that sets adoption back; a visible quick win builds the case for the next phase.

Principle 1: Data-driven intent mapping

The first flows to migrate are chosen from evidence. Unsupervised LLM clustering runs over historical support transcripts and groups them by what employees actually asked for, rather than by the categories the old menu assumed.

The output is a ranked map of intents. High-volume, high-friction, well-understood flows go first, and VPN troubleshooting and MFA resets are the expected candidates. They are frequent, procedural and already documented, so they deliver visible return on investment quickly with the lowest risk.

Clustering also surfaces what the menu never captured: intents nobody planned for, and the long tail that should stay with human agents.

Principle 2: Zero-hallucination guardrails

The concierge answers only from what the bank has already verified. A Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) framework grounds every response strictly in JPMC's internal standard operating procedures (SOPs). If no approved source supports an answer, the system doesn't improvise; it hands off.

A "Privacy Gate" sits in front of the model. It masks PII in the transcript before any text reaches an LLM, so the model reasons over placeholders and never sees raw identifiers.

Together, the two controls separate what the system may say (verified SOPs only) from what it may see (masked data only). Both can be audited independently.

Principle 3: Frictionless human handoffs

AI should never trap an employee in a loop. A "Warm Handoff" passes structured intent and sentiment data directly to a human agent in ServiceNow or Genesys, so the agent starts from the problem rather than from "How can I help you?"

The handoff carries three things:

  • What the employee wants: the detected intent and what has already been tried.
  • How they feel: a sentiment signal, so frustrated callers get priority and a calmer tone.
  • Where it happened: the conversation context, so no one repeats themselves.

This also makes escalation a feature rather than a failure. Every handoff is a labelled example that can sharpen intent mapping in the next cycle.

System design: how a call flows

The three principles act at different points in one path. The Privacy Gate runs before any model sees the call, retrieval limits what the model may say, and the handoff catches everything the SOPs can't answer, along with callers the answer didn't help.

Phased migration roadmap

The new concierge runs beside the legacy IVR, proves itself on a few high-volume flows, and takes over more only after each gate (the diamonds) is met. Human agents stay in the loop throughout.

Takeaways for legacy migration at scale

Legacy migration in a regulated enterprise succeeds when it earns trust in small, measurable steps. Three lessons carry beyond this case:

  1. Let the data choose the starting point. Usage evidence beats assumptions about what users need.
  2. Design the constraints in, not on. Grounding and privacy controls are part of the architecture from day one, not a review at the end.
  3. Keep humans in the loop by design. A graceful exit to a person protects users when automation falls short and feeds the next improvement.

What I would validate next

This is a proposal, so its claims are design arguments, not measured results. Before a pilot, three things need evidence:

  • Cluster quality. Do the transcript clusters match how experienced agents would group the same calls?
  • Grounding coverage. What share of VPN and MFA calls can the verified SOPs answer without a handoff?
  • Handoff value. Does passing intent and sentiment measurably shorten agent handle time compared with a cold transfer?
